A long time ago, in a land, far far away ... The Cosmic Spark Rajesh’s stomach rumbled loudly. It was the third day in a row in which he did not have food. He missed his favourite vada pav, which he had once in a blue moon. “Can we have something to eat?”, his children would always ask, their hands rubbing their stomachs. “We don't have food. I am struggling to make ends meet.” This was Rajesh’s heartbreaking reply. The greed and ambition of the rich have caused a huge gap between the rich and the poor. But he knew that he had to do something. The next day, he woke up early. He walked a mile to the local water tap and stood in the queue. While standing there, he looked around. He saw shacks, houses built of cardboard and pipes, dirty streets, and garbage all around. 40 minutes later, after sweating in the scorching sun, Rajesh held his bucket under the tap and gently opened it. Drip. Drip. Drip. The water filled barely one-fourth of the bucket before the tap creaked to a sudden stop. Dropping a tiny, one-rupee bar of soap into the shallow water, he wiped his forehead with a ripped cloth and walked toward the bustling main road to stand by the traffic lights. As he walked, he reflected on the poverty cycle he was stuck in: generations of poverty, his lack of education, his unemployment, and how it affected his family's future. He left Dharavi and stood beside a traffic junction, ready to earn some money. 2 hours went by. No one wanted to get their car’s windshield cleaned. Rajesh closed his eyes. He hoped to earn something. But as he waited, he noticed something: the sweat, the heat, people rolling down their car windows, the swaying of newspapers, the number of people walking on the road. A car honked after it stopped at a red light. Rajesh hurriedly yet neatly wiped the windshield, thereby earning 10 rupees. Rajesh’s heart leapt in joy. He finally earned some money.
He patiently waited for the next red light. When it came, he again cleaned the car, yet this time it was a turquoise-coloured McLaren. The driver must be really rich! He'll definitely give me something. While he cleaned the car, he stared at the man wearing rags and having ripped hands, on the well-polished windshield: his reflection. The driver glanced at him once, then stared ahead and drove away when the light turned green. He didn’t react. He was blank, his eyes downcast, staring at the quarter-filled bucket with a one-rupee soap dissolved in it. The rest of the day continued. Rajesh earned Rs. 100 in total. Finally, he could return home with some food for his family. The next day, he earned only Rs. 10, barely enough to buy food. A month passed, and he only earned Rs. 1000 in total.
“This is not going to work,” Rajesh said to himself. When he reached home that evening, his wife told him that there would be a meteor shower the next day, which came once in 33 years. “There’s a saying that if you grant a wish during a meteor shower, it will come true.” She told him. “Really?”, Rajesh asked, annoyed by the superstition. That night, he couldn't sleep. Constant thoughts of doubt and reflection troubled him. Should I watch the meteor shower? Or is it just a coward’s method of blaming the universe for their purse’s emptiness? Am I a coward? No. But I am a responsible person. My family needs food and money. If my wish comes true… The next day, he stepped outside at 2 am. He was in awe. For the first time in his life, he felt carefree. His struggles had no meaning. Only he was there, and the brilliant streaks of light that illuminated the night. At last, he remembered the junction — the sweat, the rolled-down windows, the newspapers fanning people. By the next week, Rajesh had channelled that thought into action. Using discarded plastic bottles and a small motor, he built a compact fan that clipped onto any surface and ran on almost nothing. A local NGO noticed his fan and helped him produce more. The next morning, he rose before his family woke up, went to a restaurant and bought 4 plates of vada pav. When he returned home and showed it to his family, they jumped in delight. He didn’t earn as much as the wealthy in the city. It was enough to keep the family running, enough to live a middle-class life, but what he earned more was dignity, respect, and confidence.
